Malware Incident Response Playbook
This playbook is designed for an organization whose systems may be compromised and that stores customer and vendor information. Treat the incident as potentially involving both malware and unauthorized data access until investigation proves otherwise.

1. Immediate Priorities
Protect people and safety-critical operations.
Contain attacker access without destroying evidence.
Preserve logs and forensic evidence.
Determine whether customer or vendor data was accessed or exfiltrated.
Restore operations from trusted systems.
Meet legal, contractual, regulatory, and insurance obligations.
If the malware is actively encrypting files, deleting backups, spreading, or communicating with an attacker, escalate immediately to your incident-response provider, cyber insurer, legal counsel, and relevant authorities.

2. First 60 Minutes
Activate the incident team
Assign an incident commander and create a dedicated communication channel that is not dependent on potentially compromised systems.

Recommended roles:

Incident commander
IT and infrastructure lead
Security/forensics lead
Legal and privacy counsel
Executive decision-maker
Communications/customer-support lead
HR representative, if employee accounts or devices are involved
Cyber-insurance and external incident-response contacts
Record all decisions, actions, timestamps, and people involved.

Contain the suspected systems
Depending on the situation:

Isolate infected endpoints from the network using EDR or network controls.
Disconnect systems that are actively spreading malware.
Disable compromised VPN, remote-access, and administrative accounts.
Block known malicious domains, IP addresses, URLs, and file hashes.
Restrict east-west traffic between network segments.
Temporarily restrict access to customer, vendor, payment, and identity systems.
Preserve systems in their current state when possible.
Do not immediately wipe, reimage, or power off every affected device. This may destroy memory, malware, persistence mechanisms, and other evidence. If a system is actively encrypting data or causing operational harm, containment takes priority; document what was done and why.

Protect accounts and privileged access
Disable confirmed compromised accounts.
Reset passwords for affected users and administrators.
Revoke active sessions, refresh tokens, API keys, SSH keys, and cloud access tokens.
Rotate privileged, service, database, backup, and domain-administrator credentials.
Enforce multifactor authentication, especially for remote and privileged access.
Review newly created accounts, privilege changes, mailbox forwarding rules, and suspicious OAuth applications.
Do not reset credentials in a way that destroys useful authentication evidence without first consulting the forensic lead.

3. First 4 Hours
Preserve evidence
Collect and preserve, using forensic procedures where possible:

EDR alerts and endpoint timelines
Memory captures from key compromised systems
Disk images or forensic collections
Malware samples and cryptographic hashes
Email messages and malicious attachments
Authentication, VPN, firewall, DNS, proxy, and network-flow logs
Cloud identity and audit logs
File-access and database audit logs
Backup-system logs
DLP and secure web gateway alerts
System, application, and security event logs
Relevant screenshots and user reports
Preserve the original evidence as read-only and work from copies. Maintain a chain-of-custody record.

Identify the attack scope
Build an asset and account list covering:

Infected endpoints
Servers and virtual machines
Domain controllers and identity providers
Cloud accounts and storage
Databases
File shares
Backup infrastructure
Email systems
Remote-access tools
Payment and financial systems
Customer and vendor portals
Third-party integrations
Determine:

How the malware entered
The earliest known compromise time
Whether it is still active
Which accounts were used
Which systems were accessed
Whether privilege escalation occurred
Whether sensitive data was viewed, copied, compressed, or transferred
Do not assume that a lack of alerts means that data was not accessed.

4. Data-Breach Assessment
Create a data inventory for customer and vendor information.

Identify potentially exposed data
Examples include:

Names and contact information
Account identifiers
Government or tax identification numbers
Financial-account or payment information
Credit-card information
Authentication credentials
Contracts, invoices, and purchase records
Vendor banking details
Employee or contractor information
Health, insurance, or other regulated information
Proprietary pricing, product, or business data
For each data category, record:

Field	Example
Data type	Vendor bank-account details
Storage location	ERP database
Systems accessed	ERP application server
Evidence of access	Database audit log
Evidence of exfiltration	Outbound transfer to unknown host
Date range	4–9 August
Number of records	Under investigation
Applicable obligations	Contractual, privacy, financial-sector, or other
Classify findings separately as:

Confirmed accessed
Confirmed exfiltrated
Potentially exposed
Not affected based on current evidence
Unknown
Avoid describing the incident publicly as a confirmed breach until the evidence and legal review support that conclusion, but do not delay required notifications while waiting for every detail.

5. Malware Analysis and Eradication
Analyze the malware
Determine:

Malware family and capabilities
Persistence mechanisms
C2 destinations and protocols
Credential-stealing behavior
Lateral-movement tools
Data-staging locations
Encryption or destructive functionality
Whether the malware has multiple variants or payloads
Search the environment for:

Matching hashes and filenames
Scheduled tasks and services
Startup and registry modifications
Suspicious scripts and PowerShell activity
Unauthorized remote-access tools
New local or domain accounts
Unusual administrative-group membership
Suspicious archive files
C2 connections
Security-tool exclusions
Modified backup jobs or retention policies
Eradicate
Remove all identified persistence.
Reimage systems that cannot be trusted.
Patch exploited vulnerabilities.
Replace compromised credentials and secrets.
Rebuild critical identity systems if domain-level compromise is suspected.
Remove unauthorized tools and accounts.
Validate the integrity of security controls.
Scan all systems, including backups and dormant assets.
Obtain forensic confirmation before declaring eradication.

6. Backup and Recovery Procedure
Before restoring:

Determine whether backups were accessed or altered.
Verify that backup credentials were not compromised.
Identify the last known-good backup.
Scan backups for malware and persistence.
Confirm that recovery points predate the attacker’s presence.
Test restoration in an isolated environment.
Rebuild identity and administrative controls first.
Restore critical services in priority order.
Monitor restored systems continuously.
Suggested restoration order:

Identity and authentication
Network controls and security monitoring
Core business and communication systems
Customer and vendor applications
File services and collaboration platforms
Less-critical endpoints and applications
Do not reconnect restored systems to the production network until security controls, credentials, logging, and monitoring are in place.

7. Communications and Notifications
Internal communications
Provide employees with clear instructions:

Do not open suspicious attachments or links.
Do not use potentially compromised systems for incident communications.
Do not delete files, emails, or logs.
Do not contact suspected attackers without authorization.
Report unusual prompts, files, encryption activity, or account behavior.
Do not discuss the incident externally or on social media.
External coordination
Engage:

Legal and privacy counsel
Cyber-insurance provider
External incident-response and forensic specialists
Relevant regulators
Law enforcement, where appropriate
Payment-card or financial-sector contacts, if applicable
Key vendors and customers, when required
Credit-monitoring or support providers, if appropriate
Notification obligations may depend on the type of data, affected jurisdictions, contractual terms, and whether access or exfiltration is confirmed. Counsel should coordinate the formal assessment and notification process.

8. Ransomware-Specific Branch
If files are encrypted or a ransom demand is present:

Isolate affected systems immediately.
Protect backup systems from further access.
Preserve the ransom note, wallet address, email address, and timestamps.
Do not negotiate or pay without executive, legal, insurance, and law-enforcement review.
Determine whether data theft occurred in addition to encryption.
Do not assume that decryption or payment resolves the compromise.
Obtain professional advice before communicating with the threat actor.
Check applicable sanctions and legal restrictions before any payment consideration.

9. Recovery Validation
Before closing the incident, verify:

All known attacker access paths are closed.
Credentials and secrets have been rotated.
Vulnerabilities used in the attack are patched or mitigated.
Endpoint, identity, network, and cloud monitoring are functioning.
No unauthorized accounts, services, scheduled tasks, or remote tools remain.
Backups are protected and recoverable.
Customer and vendor data exposure has been assessed.
Required notifications have been completed.
Business owners have accepted residual risk.
A final incident report and lessons-learned review are complete.
Maintain heightened monitoring for at least several weeks after recovery, or longer if advised by your incident-response provider.
